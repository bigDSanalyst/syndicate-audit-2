# When to retire: the cultural evolution of post-reproductive oblique teaching

## Abstract

> Teaching is ubiquitous across human societies. Instruction from parents to offspring is the most
> fundamental form of teaching, explained primarily through kin selection and inclusive fitness.
> Instruction to non-kin, known as oblique teaching, also plays a dominant role in modern
> societies, where teaching is often institutionalized. Nonetheless, it remains puzzling how or
> when oblique teaching can evolve. Here we address the evolution of oblique teaching by
> developing a mathematical model of stage-structured populations. Younger individuals engage in a
> useful activity, called hunting, and reproduce. At some point in life, they cease contributing
> directly to hunting and reproduction, and instead serve exclusively as teachers who increase the
> hunting expertise of the young. We refer to this transition in life stage as retirement, and we
> analyze the evolution of the retirement rate, and the resulting frequency of post-reproductive
> teachers, in a population subject to a global carrying capacity. We find that retirement and
> teaching can evolve without kinship or inclusive fitness, but only in populations above a
> threshold size. Unlike in classical adaptive dynamics, the evolutionarily stable retirement
> rates depend on the absolute population size of a mutant type upon introduction. In many
> regimes, evolution produces fewer teachers than would maximize the population's per-capita
> hunting returns or intrinsic birth rate. A mutant that differs only slightly from the resident
> type generally cannot invade from a minority, so teaching cannot emerge through gradual
> evolution. The mechanism we describe is therefore available to cultural evolution, where a
> distinct norm for retirement and elder care may be adopted by a sub-population simultaneously.
> Our account is thus tied closely to theories of cultural evolution, including the "information
> grandmother hypothesis" of Henrich and Henrich.
